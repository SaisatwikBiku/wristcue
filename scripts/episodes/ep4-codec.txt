Sound in the air is a smooth change in pressure. A microphone turns that pressure into a changing voltage, and a converter measures the voltage many thousands of times per second. Each measurement is a sample. Compact discs, for example, use forty-four thousand one hundred samples per second, with sixteen bits for each one.

Stored that way, audio is large. A few minutes of uncompressed sound takes tens of megabytes. That's too much for a podcast feed, and far too much for a watch with limited storage.

AAC, which stands for Advanced Audio Coding, makes the file smaller by throwing away what you're unlikely to hear. It splits the sound into short frames, looks at which frequencies are present in each one, and spends its bits on the parts your ear pays the most attention to. Quiet sounds next to loud ones, and very high frequencies, get fewer bits or none.

That's why AAC is called lossy. You can't get the original samples back. For speech, which uses a narrower range of frequencies than music, you can remove a great deal before anyone notices.
